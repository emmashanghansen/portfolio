Rural doctors can't diagnose what they can't see

For patients in rural areas, a potentially life-threatening condition can go undetected for years. The nearest specialist is hours away. New handheld ultrasound could change that, but most doctors have never been trained to use it. SINTEF is developing an AI to detect, measure, and guide doctors through aortic aneurysm examinations in real time, and brought me in to design the interface from scratch.

I started from zero, just like the users

I came in with no ultrasound knowledge. I observed experts, practised on colleagues, and briefly wondered where the challenge was. Until I interviewed GPs. Unlike me, they couldn't afford to experiment. A misdiagnosis has real consequences. Teaching the procedure was only half the problem. The harder part was helping them feel confident in their decisions.

"We're scared to misdiagnose the patient."

Process photo: expert interview or observation session

Five steps every expert agreed on

Experts call ultrasound an art, not a science. Every one I spoke with did it differently. Across all of them, I identified five steps that were always present.

1 Place probe · 2 Find aorta · 3 Scan aorta · 4 Measure short axis · 5 Measure long axis

The AI wasn't built yet. I designed anyway.

An AI still in training is unpredictable. We didn't know exactly what it would be capable of, or how it would behave. Instead of waiting, I simulated its behaviour through storyboards and early prototypes, testing ideas with GPs. This also let me feed back into the AI's development. Where users struggled most directly informed what the AI needed to learn. When it failed to identify smaller, healthier aortas, we prioritised training it on more varied patient data.

Two prototypes instead of one

Doctors wanted a fully automated, clinically approved tool. Legal constraints made that impossible, with the AI not yet approved for clinical decisions. So I pushed for two prototypes: a testable MVP the development team could realistically build and use in SINTEF's trials, and a video prototype exploring the features doctors actually needed. It kept the team focused and gave us richer feedback than a single prototype would have.

Three features that took the most attempts

Exploration 01

Users couldn't tell when they had completed a step

Users moved through the procedure with uncertainty, pausing often and looking to us for confirmation that they were doing the right thing.

What if the system kept track of where they were, so the doctor didn't have to?

Visuals · tested with 12 GPs and 2 clinical experts

Exploration 02

I disagreed with a project manager on where to place the guidance

We had different hypotheses. I pushed for testing both with users.

Exploration 03

Knowing when to stop and measure requires experience most GPs don't have

The doctor's focus is on the ultrasound. Any signal to freeze and measure needs to be understood at a glance.

The final result

A guided workflow for AAA examination, designed to support a GP from probe placement to final measurement.

The interface had to work in a specific physical context. A laptop to the left of the GP, a patient on the right, and a probe in one hand. Every design decision was made with that room in mind.

Throughout the workflow, the AI suggests and guides, but the GP always makes the final call. That was a deliberate choice.

This project made me a flexible collaborator

Involving the developers in design decisions early made them invested in the outcome. The design stopped being mine and became a shared effort.

Testing with medical professionals changed how I prepare. Their time is limited, so every session had to count.

I learned to adapt my methods. The ideal testing setup wasn't always available, so I found ways to get value from imperfect conditions instead of waiting for perfect ones.

The prototype is being used in SINTEF's 2026 studies. At handover, I wrote a structured summary of the final testing findings for the doctorate student continuing the work.
